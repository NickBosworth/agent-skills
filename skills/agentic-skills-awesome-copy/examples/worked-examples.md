# Original worked examples

Everything below is synthetic, including product behavior, dates and measurements.
These are editorial demonstrations, not customer evidence or executed model trials.
Each improved version uses only its stated brief; none is a universal house style.

## 1. Make a product promise concrete

**Brief:** A planning tool displays unassigned shifts for a small cafe. Managers
can assign them in a weekly view. No time-saving study or automation is supplied.

**Before:** "Unlock the power of effortless team management with our seamless,
next-generation scheduling solution."

**After:**

> See which shifts still need someone.
>
> Assign them from the weekly view before you share the rota.

**Why:** The headline gives the reader a real use. The support line explains it.
"Before you share" describes an order of work, without claiming the tool can
publish the rota. No automatic scheduling or measured speed is invented.

## 2. Choose different title angles

**Brief:** An essay describes how packing one small bag makes the author choose
what matters. It includes a packing checklist. Offer three titles.

- "What fits in one small bag" — a concrete image.
- "Packing less means choosing more carefully" — the essay's observation.
- "A packing list for one small bag" — the practical angle.

**Why:** The titles make different promises. The first suits a reflective essay;
the third suits a reader looking for the checklist. "The ultimate guide to
unlocking minimalist travel" would introduce a different and much larger promise.

## 3. Give a paragraph movement without a fake anecdote

**Brief:** A fictional pottery newsletter announces that bowls fired on Monday
are available on Thursday. The batch has pale green glaze; variation is visible.

**Before:** "We are thrilled to unveil our exquisite new collection, celebrating
the beauty of craftsmanship and offering something truly special for everyone."

**After:**

> Monday's firing is ready for Thursday's shelves. This batch is pale green,
> with small differences in the glaze from bowl to bowl. Choose the one you like.

**Why:** The supplied process and visible detail carry the character. There is no
invented origin story, handmade claim, scarcity or description of the reader's life.

## 4. Keep a formal note formal

**Brief:** Write a courteous notice for a research reading room. It closes at
16:00 on 22 October for maintenance; ordinary hours return the next day.

**Before:** "We wish to inform all valued visitors that, due to essential
maintenance activities, an adjustment to our operational schedule will occur."

**After:**

> The reading room will close at 16:00 on 22 October for maintenance.
> Normal opening hours resume on 23 October.

**Why:** The useful information leads. There is no need to add slang, jokes or
contractions to make this notice sound like a person wrote it.

## 5. Describe a failure without false reassurance

**Brief:** An upload has failed. The interface offers a retry, but the cause and
whether any progress was saved are unknown.

**Before:** "Oops! A tiny hiccup got in the way, but don't worry—your work is safe."

**After:**

> The upload failed. Try uploading the file again.

**Why:** It names the known state and the supported recovery. It does not invent
a network problem, preserved progress or a trivial level of impact.

## 6. Match a CTA to the next screen

**Brief:** A button opens a sample report. It does not create an account or start
a trial. Maximum label length: 24 characters, including spaces.

**Before:** "Transform your workflow"

**After:** "View sample report" — 18 characters.

**Why:** The label tells the reader what will happen. Its clarity is useful even
though the phrase is conventional. A novelty requirement would make it worse.

## 7. Keep the qualification

**Brief:** In a fictional pilot, 18 of 24 surveyed participants said they found
their files faster. Results were self-reported. Rewrite for a short report.

**Before:** "Our platform revolutionizes productivity, helping users locate files
faster than ever before."

**After:**

> In the pilot survey, 18 of 24 participants said they found files faster.

**Why:** The wording keeps the sample and the self-report. It does not turn an
observation into measured speed, universal performance or causal proof.

## 8. Keep an already effective line

**Brief:** A saved draft can be edited before sending. Review the copy for generic
phrasing while preserving the supplied voice.

**Original:** "Your draft is saved—you can change it before you send it."

**Decision:** Keep it.

**Why:** The line gives an accurate state and a relevant next step. Its dash and
contraction do useful work. Neither is evidence of a writing defect or authorship.

## 9. Preserve warmth with a small joke

**Brief:** A fictional garden shop sells a compact watering can that fits on a
windowsill. Use a gently playful voice. No automatic watering is offered.

**Before:** "Elevate your urban oasis with an innovative watering experience."

**After:**

> A watering can that fits on the windowsill.
>
> It still needs you to do the watering.

**Why:** A supplied detail and an honest limitation support a restrained joke.
This voice would be inappropriate for a payment error or a solemn notice.

## 10. Make international instructions literal

**Brief:** Write English help text for an international audience. People must
enter an order number to check delivery status. No deadline is supplied.

**Before:** "Let's get the ball rolling—pop your number in and you're golden."

**After:**

> Enter your order number to check the delivery status.

**Why:** The action and object are explicit. Removing idiom improves this context;
it does not mean idioms are wrong in every audience's writing.

## 11. Finish where the argument arrives

**Brief:** An internal note explains that unclear task ownership delayed a fictional
review. It recommends naming one decision owner before the next review.

**Before:** "Ultimately, by fostering a culture of collaboration, we can unlock
a brighter future and take our team to new heights."

**After:**

> Before the next review, name the person who will make the decision.

**Why:** The ending follows from the stated problem and gives the supported next
step. It does not recap the note or add a broad aspiration that the note cannot earn.
